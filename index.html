A $6 coloring book is a unit-economics problem

A note from Extra Hustles

AI-generated coloring books get pitched as a design shortcut: generate the pages, upload, collect royalties. The Amazon KDP listing view shows a royalty per sale, and a $6.99 cover looks like easy margin. The unit that decides whether the hustle pays is the printed copy, because every copy carries a print cost and an ad cost that the cover price has to beat.

KDP prints on demand, so there is no inventory to buy, and no bulk discount either. On paperbacks KDP pays 60 percent of list minus the print cost, and print cost climbs with page count. Suppose the book runs 100 pages at a $3 print cost and lists at $6.99. The royalty works out to about $1.19 a copy. A hundred copies in a month is $119, which sounds fine until the ads enter.

Amazon ads charge per click. Suppose a click runs 35 cents and one sale in ten clicks converts: ten clicks cost $3.50 to produce one sale, and the $1.19 royalty comes up $2.31 short. Lifting the cover price to $9.99 raises the royalty to $2.99, still under the ad bill. The math clears near $12.99, where the royalty reaches $4.79 and leaves about $1.29 per ad-driven sale.

What saves it is that design hours amortize. Call it 12 hours to generate, order, and proof a 100-page book. At $6.99 with no ad spend, a hundred copies a month pays $9.95 an hour in month one. If the book holds a hundred copies a month for six months, the same 12 hours have returned $714, about $59.70 an hour. Gig apps pay weekly and can zero out on one deactivation. A KDP listing pays slowly and keeps paying while it lives, so its hourly rate only makes sense across months.

Price the run before drawing page one. At a $6.99 list the ad lane stays closed no matter how thin the book ships: a 24-page edition at a $1 print cost still nets $3.19 a copy against the same $3.50 ad bill. The copy math says pick the niche for organic traffic, set the price against print cost, and treat ads as a scale decision that only pays above $12.99.
